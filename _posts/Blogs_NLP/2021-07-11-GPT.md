You probably already know that a large part of the BERT paper is devoted to comparisons with GPT and ELMo. You also know that GPT is an autoregressive language model, and that XLNet, a permutation autoregressive language model, was inspired by the GPT family of models.  
GPT came out between the Transformer and BERT, but it means more than just "a language model built from the decoder."  
The abstract begins with  
> Natural language understanding comprises a wide range of diverse tasks such as textual entailment, question answering, semantic similarity assessment, and document classification. Although large unlabeled text corpora are abundant, <span style="color:orange">labeled data for learning these specific tasks is scarce, making it challenging for discriminatively trained models to perform adequately.</span> 

In other words:  
"Natural language understanding covers a very wide range of tasks, such as textual entailment, question answering, semantic similarity assessment, and document classification. Even though unlabeled corpora are extremely abundant, <u>the scarcity of labeled data for each specific task is an obstacle to models trained discriminatively (specialized) for each task performing well.</u>"  
Put simply, NLP has a huge number of tasks, and as a result <span style="color:orange">labeled data for any individual task is only a tiny fraction of what's available</span>. That is the problem.  
It's also easy to guess that the goal of this paper is to <span style="color:orange">make task-specific models work well despite this problem</span>. 
And so:  
> We demonstrate that large gains on these tasks can be realized by generative pre-training of a language model on a diverse corpus of unlabeled text, followed by discriminative fine-tuning on each specific task.   

"Gains on these NLU tasks can be achieved by generative pre-training of a language model on a diverse unlabeled corpus, followed by discriminative fine-tuning on each specific task."  
    
This is the <span style="color:orange">pre-train & fine-tune</span> methodology of standing on the shoulders of giants, which we saw in BERT. The GPT paper came out before BERT, and BERT cites it in the very first sentence of its introduction.  
> Language model pre-training has been shown to be effective for improving many natural language processing tasks (Dai and Le, 2015; Peters et al., 2018a; <span style="color:green">Radford et al., 2018</span>; Howard and Ruder, 2018).     

Looking closely at the citation list, Dai and Le, 2015 is there, which shows that the idea of pre-training & fine-tuning in NLP already existed in 2015.  
Back to the abstract of the GPT paper:  
> In contrast to previous approaches, we make <span style="color:orange">use of task-aware input transformations during fine-tuning</span> to achieve effective transfer while requiring minimal changes to the model architecture. We demonstrate the effectiveness of our approach on a wide range of benchmarks for natural language understanding.   

The second sentence describes the effectiveness of the approach they chose, generative pre-training + discriminative fine-tuning, and says it worked across a wide range of NLU benchmarks. "Task-aware input transformation" means reshaping the input into a form suited to the downstream task during fine-tuning. This is explained in Section 3.3 of the paper.  

> Our general task-agnostic model outperforms discriminatively trained models that use architectures specifically crafted for each task, significantly improving upon the state of the art in 9 out of the 12 tasks studied. For instance, we achieve absolute improvements of 8.9% on commonsense reasoning (Stories Cloze Test), 5.7% on question answering (RACE), and 1.5% on textual entailment (MultiNLI).  
  
This sentence explains what the gains on the benchmarks were. You can think of the "general task-agnostic model" as the pre-trained + fine-tuned model, which is the GPT model itself. It outperformed models with architectures designed and trained separately for each task, improving so much that it set the state of the art on 9 of the 12 tasks. For example, it improved by 8.9% on the Stories Cloze Test (commonsense reasoning), 5.7% on RACE (QA), and 1.5% on MultiNLI (textual entailment).  

Having read the abstract this far, the topic of the paper seems to be that <span style="color:orange">generative pre-training (GPT) + discriminative fine-tuning is an effective methodology</span>, just like the other NLP pre-training models we've seen. But is that really all? Again, this paper came out before BERT.
