## 1. Overview

[GitHub - openai/gpt-2: Code for the paper "Language Models are Unsupervised Multitask Learners"](https://github.com/openai/gpt-2)

Looking at it from the outside, the difference between when GPT first came out and when this paper came out (late 2018 to early 2019) is the existence of BERT. Yet GPT-2 is surprisingly uninterested in comparing itself with BERT.

Rather than indifference, the real reason is that BERT, which assumes fine-tuning, and GPT-2, which puts its emphasis on pre-training, are hard to compare objectively. And even if you did compare them, it wouldn't be a contest (as you'll see later in this post).

Building on the zero-shot behavior of pre-trained models that we saw in [GPT](/Blogs/NLP/GPT/), GPT-2 aims to strengthen the model's general ability, that is, the ability to do downstream tasks without any separate fine-tuning.

Put another way, the GPT series has a consistent goal: to make a model understand language itself through language-model pre-training.

The paper's title says it outright: "Language Models are Unsupervised Multitask Learners."

It started with Generative Pre-Training + Supervised Fine-tuning (GPT),

and with GPT-2 they nailed it down as an UNSUPERVISED Multitask Learner.

As anyone who has read the paper will feel, it is loosely structured and long-winded because it has so much it wants to say. In particular, the abstracts of both GPT and GPT-2 do not faithfully capture the content of the whole paper, which makes them hard to read. So I have tried to reorganize it as systematically as possible by topic. Please bear with me.

OpenAI's research goal can be seen as building an unsupervised pre-trained language model so good that supervised fine-tuning becomes unnecessary.

<div class="notice--info" markdown="1">
💡 Google, Facebook and others of course acknowledge the importance of pre-training. But their position is that however good a pre-trained model is, what it does is make the fine-tuning network lighter and less expensive. It is not a cure-all, and for NLP tasks fine-tuning (supervised learning) is a must.
</div>

Did OpenAI achieve this bold goal with GPT-2, which slightly changes GPT's layers and makes it enormously bigger? Let's find out.

## 2. Abstract

Let's look at the abstract.

> Natural language processing tasks, such as question answering, machine translation, reading comprehension, and summarization, are typically approached with supervised learning on task-specific datasets. We demonstrate that language models begin to learn these tasks without any explicit supervision when trained on a new dataset of millions of webpages called WebText.

Right from the start comes a startling claim. A language model, that is, the model being pre-trained, can learn how to do tasks (machine translation, question answering, reading comprehension and so on) without any explicit instruction. How? By pre-training it on a dataset of millions of web pages called WebText.

If picking the right dataset for pre-training means you don't need separate supervised fine-tuning, let's take a closer look at what this WebText data actually is.

Section 2.1 Training Dataset first discusses Common Crawl, a web-crawl dataset, and the problems it has. Then:

> Instead, we created a new web scrape which emphasizes document quality. To do this we only scraped web pages which have been curated/filtered by humans. Manually filtering a full web scrape would be exceptionally expensive so as a starting point, we scraped all outbound links from Reddit, a social media platform, which received at least 3 karma. This can be thought of as a heuristic indicator for whether other users found the link interesting, educational, or just funny.

They built the dataset by collecting only high-quality web pages. Because having humans review everything from start to finish would be enormously expensive, they collected outbound links on Reddit that received at least 3 karma.

> The resulting dataset, WebText, contains the text subset of these 45 million links. To extract the text from HTML responses we use a combination of the Dragnet (Peters & Lecocq, 2013) and Newspaper content extractors. All results presented in this paper use a preliminary version of WebText which does not include links created after Dec 2017 and which after de-duplication and some heuristic based cleaning contains slightly over 8 million documents for a total of 40 GB of text. We removed all Wikipedia documents from WebText since it is a common data source for other datasets and could complicate analysis due to overlapping training data with test evaluation tasks.

The resulting dataset, WebText, consists of text extracted from the HTML documents behind 45 million links. Documents created up to December 2017 were used to train the models in this paper. After de-duplication and cleaning there are slightly over 8 million documents, 40 GB in total. Since Wikipedia is a source commonly used by other datasets, all Wikipedia documents were removed from WebText to prevent overlapping data from remaining during evaluation.

Going back to the abstract, there is one more key point in this research: model size.

> The capacity of the language model is essential to the success of zero-shot task transfer and increasing it improves performance in a log-linear fashion across tasks. Our largest model, GPT-2, is a 1.5B parameter Transformer that achieves state of the art results on 7 out of 8 tested language modeling datasets in a zero-shot setting but still underfits WebText.

Zero-shot task transfer means having a pre-trained model perform a task without fine-tuning. For this to succeed, they say, model size is important.

Their largest model, GPT-2, is a Transformer with 1.5 billion parameters, and they say it hit SOTA zero-shot on 7 of 8 tasks!!!!!

![GPT vs GPT-2 architecture](/assets/img_nlp/GPT2_GPT.png)

> We use a Transformer (Vaswani et al., 2017) based architecture for our LMs. The model largely follows the details of the OpenAI GPT model (Radford et al., 2018) with a few modifications. Layer normalization (Ba et al., 2016) was moved to the input of each sub-block, similar to a pre-activation residual network (He et al., 2016) and an additional layer normalization was added after the final self-attention block. A modified initialization which accounts for the accumulation on the residual path with model depth is used. We scale the weights of residual layers at initialization by a factor of $1/\sqrt{N}$ where $N$ is the number of residual layers. The vocabulary is expanded to 50,257. We also increase the context size from 512 to 1024 tokens and a larger batchsize of 512 is used.

As the figure shows, the position of Layer Norm changed. In each block, Layer Norm is applied first and then the input goes into self-attention, and after the very last block a final Layer Norm is applied.

## 4. Metrics

Next are the metrics you need to know to understand the paper's experimental results.

### 4-1. Perplexity

Perplexity comes up often in the paper for comparing performance, so it is worth understanding properly.

Let's remind ourselves of the definition of a language model:

the probability that certain tokens in a corpus form a sequence in a particular order.

The probability that $u\_{11}, u\_{12}, u\_{13}$, which we saw in the [GPT](/Blogs/NLP/GPT/) post, form a sequence can be written with an autoregressive model as follows.

$$
P(u_{11}, u_{12}, u_{13})=P(u_{11})P(u_{12}|u_{11})P(u_{13}|u_{11}, u_{12})
$$

As a side note, you can also do language modeling with an N-gram model, which uses the conditional probability of a token appearing after the previous N-1 tokens. For example, a bi-gram language model looks like this.

$$
P(u_{11}, u_{12}, u_{13})=P(u_{11})P(u_{12}|u_{11})P(u_{13}|u_{12})
$$

Anyway, assuming $u\_{11}, u\_{12}, u\_{13}$ are all the tokens of the test dataset, the perplexity of $P(u\_{11}, u\_{12}, u\_{13})$, the result of language-modeling the entire test data, is:

$$
Perplexity=\sqrt[3\,]{\frac{1}{P(u_{11}, u_{12}, u_{13})}}
$$

It is the inverse of the probability, i.e. of the language model, normalized to a per-token measure. The higher the probability (the better the language model fits the test data), the lower the perplexity.

Perplexity is also defined as $2^{H}$, where $H$ is the entropy, and the formula above can be derived from it. The derivation is easy to find online, so I won't cover it here.

### 4-2. BLEU Score

One of the most widely used metrics for evaluating machine translation quality is BLEU (Bilingual Evaluation Understudy).

To compute a BLEU score,

you have a candidate sequence being evaluated (the model's output) and reference sequences to compare against, and you count how many of the N-grams that make up the candidate appear in the references.

$$
BLEU\ score = \frac{\text{total number of N-grams appearing in both the references and the candidate}}{\text{total number of words in the candidate}}
$$

For example, with

Candidate: I play soccer

Reference 1: I eat breakfast

Reference 2: She watches soccer

counting uni-grams,

"I" and "soccer" each appear at least once in the references, and the candidate has 3 uni-grams in total, so

$$
BLEU = \frac{2}{3}=0.667
$$

As you can see, BLEU scores by how often words appear and ignores semantic meaning and grammatical structure, so its limitations are clear.

For

Candidate: soccer I play

Reference: I play soccer

the BLEU score is 1.

---

### 4-3. ROUGE (Recall-Oriented Understudy for Gisting Evaluation)

This metric was originally introduced for machine summarization, but it is also used for machine translation.

Like BLEU, ROUGE compares the N-gram overlap between the machine text produced by the model and a human-written reference text. The difference is that it computes recall and precision, defined as follows.

$$
Recall=\frac{\text{total number of N-grams overlapping between model and reference}}{\text{total number of words in the reference}}
$$

$$
Precision=\frac{\text{total number of N-grams overlapping between model and reference}}{\text{total number of words in the model text}}
$$

After computing recall and precision, the final ROUGE score is calculated with the F-measure.

Computing it with uni-gram overlap is called ROUGE-1,

and with bi-gram overlap ROUGE-2.

Computing it from the length of the overlapping LCS (Longest Common Subsequence) is called ROUGE-L.

Let's compute it with the example below.

Model Text: The cat had striped orange fur

Reference Text: The cat had orange fur

The uni-gram overlap is "The", "cat", "had", "orange", "fur".

In this case recall is 1, precision is $\frac{5}{6}$, and ROUGE-1 is $2\cdot \frac{1\cdot \frac{5}{6}}{1+\frac{5}{6}}=0.91$

The bi-gram overlap is "The cat", "cat had", "orange fur".

In this case recall is $\frac{3}{4}$, precision is $\frac{3}{5}$, and ROUGE-2 is $2\cdot \frac{\frac{3}{4}\cdot \frac{3}{5}}{\frac{3}{4}+\frac{3}{5}}=0.67$

The LCS overlap is "The cat had orange fur".

In this case recall is 1, precision is $\frac{5}{6}$, and ROUGE-L is $2\cdot \frac{1\cdot \frac{5}{6}}{1+\frac{5}{6}}=0.91$

### 5-1. T5

Remember Google's T5?

T5 came out almost a year after GPT-2. I recommend recalling how T5 did multi-task learning and comparing the two.

Below is the part of the T5 paper that cites GPT-2.

> Radford et al. (2019) evaluate the zero-shot learning capabilities of language models by feeding some input to the model as a prefix and then autoregressively sampling an output. For example, automatic summarization is done by feeding in a document followed by the text “TL;DR:” (short for “too long, didn’t read”, a common abbreviation) and then the summary is predicted via autoregressive decoding. We mainly consider models that explicitly process an input with an encoder before generating an output with a separate decoder and we focus on transfer learning rather than zero-shot learning.

T5 (like BERT) acknowledges the importance of pre-trained models but does not give up on fine-tuning.

> Language models are typically used for compression or sequence generation (Graves, 2013). However, they can also be used in the text-to-text framework simply by concatenating the inputs and targets. As an example, consider the case of English to German translation: If we have a training datapoint with input sentence “That is good.” and target “Das ist gut.”, we would simply train the model on next-step prediction over the concatenated input sequence “translate English to German: That is good. target: Das ist gut.” If we wanted to obtain the model’s prediction for this example, the model would be fed the prefix “translate English to German: That is good. target:” and would be asked to generate the remainder of the sequence autoregressively. In this way, the model can predict an output sequence given an input, which satisfies the needs of text-to-text tasks. This approach was recently used to show that language models can learn to perform some text-to-text tasks without supervision (Radford et al., 2019)

T5, under the name text-to-text, specified which task to perform through a text prefix such as "translate English to German:". GPT-2 used a similar method, and because they are similar, the later T5 paper cites it.

### 5-3. How the Tasks Are Performed

<div class="notice--info" markdown="1">
💡 If all you're given is a bare pre-trained model, how on earth do you do a task and evaluate it? They said they did it zero-shot, without supervised fine-tuning.
</div>

#### Reading Comprehension

> Greedy decoding from GPT-2 when conditioned on a document, the history of the associated conversation, and a final token `A:` achieves 55 F1 on the development set. This matches or exceeds the performance of 3 out of 4 baseline systems without using the 127,000+ manually collected question answer pairs those baselines were trained on.

The input to GPT-2 is the document, followed by the associated conversation history, followed by the token `A:`. The output tokens are produced by greedy decoding.

#### Summarization

> We test GPT-2’s ability to perform summarization on the CNN and Daily Mail dataset (Nallapati et al., 2016). To induce summarization behavior we add the text `TL;DR:` after the article and generate 100 tokens with Top-*k* random sampling (Fan et al., 2018) with k = 2 which reduces repetition and encourages more abstractive summaries than greedy decoding. We use the first 3 generated sentences in these 100 tokens as the summary.

For summarization, the text `TL;DR:` is appended to the end of the input article. The output is 100 tokens obtained by top-2 random sampling, and the first 3 sentences of those 100 tokens are taken as the summary.

#### Translation

> We test whether GPT-2 has begun to learn how to translate from one language to another. In order to help it infer that this is the desired task, we condition the language model on a context of example pairs of the format `english sentence = french sentence` and then after a final prompt of `english sentence =` we sample from the model with greedy decoding and use the first generated sentence as the translation.

For example, to translate English to French, the context fed into the language model is built in the format "English sentence = French sentence", and then the English sentence we want translated is appended to the end of the context as "English sentence =".

The output is obtained by greedy decoding.

#### Question Answering

> A potential way to test what information is contained within a language model is to evaluate how often it generates the correct answer to factoid-style questions. Previous showcasing of this behavior in neural systems where all information is stored in parameters such as A Neural Conversational Model (Vinyals & Le, 2015) reported qualitative results due to the lack of high-quality evaluation datasets. The recently introduced Natural Questions dataset (Kwiatkowski et al., 2019) is a promising resource to test this more quantitatively. Similar to translation, the context of the language model is seeded with example question answer pairs which helps the model infer the short answer style of the dataset.

Example question-answer pairs are given as model input so that the model can infer that short answers are expected.

#### Winograd Schema Challenge

![Winograd Schema Challenge](/assets/img_nlp/GPT2_winograd.png)

> Recently Trinh & Le (2018) demonstrated significant progress on this challenge using LMs, by predicting the resolution of the ambiguity with higher probability. We follow their problem formulation and visualize the performance of our models with both full and partial scoring techniques in Figure 3. GPT-2 improves state of the art accuracy by 7%, achieving 70.70%. The dataset is quite small with only 273 examples so we recommend reading Trichelair et al. (2018) to help contextualize this result.

The method Trinh & Le of Google Brain used for the Winograd Schema Challenge in 2018 is almost the same as GPT-2's: they did the task with a large language model pre-trained without supervision.

- *The trophy doesn’t fit in the suitcase because **it** is too big. What is too big?*  
  ***Answer 0**: the trophy. **Answer 1**: the suitcase*

For the problem above, "it" is replaced with "the trophy" and with "the suitcase", and the prediction is decided by comparing the probabilities.

Comparing

$$
P(\text{The trophy doesn't fit in the suitcase because the trophy is too big})
$$

and

$$
P(\text{The trophy doesn't fit in the suitcase because the suitcase is too big})
$$

is called full scoring. Comparing the probabilities of the tokens after the substituted token,

$$
P(\text{is too big} \mid \text{The trophy doesn't fit in the suitcase because the trophy})
$$

and

$$
P(\text{is too big} \mid \text{The trophy doesn't fit in the suitcase because the suitcase})
$$

is called partial scoring.

GPT-2 performed the task the same way, and you can see that the large models outperform the SOTA.

#### Translation

> On the WMT-14 English-French test set, GPT-2 gets 5 BLEU, which is slightly worse than a word-by-word substitution with a bilingual lexicon inferred in previous work on unsupervised word translation (Conneau et al., 2017b). On the WMT-14 French-English test set, GPT-2 is able to leverage its very strong English language model to perform significantly better, achieving 11.5 BLEU. Performance on this task was surprising to us, since we deliberately removed non-English webpages from WebText as a filtering step. In order to confirm this, we ran a byte-level language detector on WebText which detected only 10MB of data in the French language which is approximately 500x smaller than the monolingual French corpus common in prior unsupervised machine translation research.

English→French translation scored 5 BLEU and French→English 11.5 BLEU. That is far below the 33.5 BLEU of the best unsupervised machine translation approach.

Their excuse is that, to keep the translation evaluation objective, they filtered non-English text out of the WebText training data as much as possible, and the French that appeared in WebText was 500 times smaller than the French corpora used in earlier unsupervised machine translation research. In other words, the model was at a disadvantage, with far less exposure to French.

## 6. Generalization vs Memorization

Above we saw the results of doing multiple tasks with nothing but a pre-trained model.

What does this mean?

Has the pre-trained model acquired general language ability?

Or is it simply remembering?

We need to think about this question.

The GPT-2 authors want to say that the GPT-2 model, pre-trained on their own WebText dataset, does not simply remember the WebText data but has a generalized ability with language.

Since proving generalization is hard, they first show that it is not memorization.

"Isn't the test data you benchmarked all in the WebText corpus you trained on? Aren't you cheating?"

"No, sir. If that were the case, it wouldn't be true zero-shot.

That's why we checked it with a Bloom filter."

[Bloom Filter](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bloom_filter)

> To study this we created Bloom filters containing 8-grams of WebText training set tokens. To improve recall, strings were normalized to contain only lower-cased alphanumeric words with a single space as a delimiter. The Bloom filters were constructed such that the false positive rate is upper bounded by $\frac{1}{10^8}$. We further verified the low false positive rate by generating 1M strings, of which zero were found by the filter.  
> These Bloom filters let us calculate, given a dataset, the percentage of 8-grams from that dataset that are also found in the WebText training set. Table 6 shows this overlap analysis for the test sets of common LM benchmarks. Common LM datasets’ test sets have between 1-6% overlap with WebText train, with an average of overlap of 3.2%. Somewhat surprisingly, many datasets have larger overlaps with their own training splits, with an average of 5.9% overlap.  
> Our approach optimizes for recall, and while manual inspection of the overlaps shows many common phrases, there are many longer matches that are due to duplicated data. This is not unique to WebText. For instance, we discovered that the test set of WikiText-103 has an article which is also in the training dataset. Since there are only 60 articles in the test set there is at least an overlap of 1.6%. Potentially more worryingly, 1BW has an overlap of nearly 13.2% with its own training set according to our procedure.

The Bloom filter was built from the 8-gram tokens of the WebText training set and shows how many of a test dataset's 8-grams overlap with WebText. The false positive rate of the filter,

$$
FPR=\frac{FP}{FP+TN}
$$

that is, the rate at which it judges an 8-gram to be in WebText when it is not, was set to one in 100 million, almost zero. They then checked how much the training set provided with each benchmark, and WebText, overlap with that benchmark's test set.

![Table 6: 8-gram overlap](/assets/img_nlp/GPT2_8-grams.png)

> Overall, our analysis suggests that data overlap between WebText training data and specific evaluation datasets provides a small but consistent benefit to reported results. However, for most datasets we do not notice significantly larger overlaps than those already existing between standard training and test sets, as Table 6 highlights.

In short, the overlap between the WebText dataset and the benchmark test sets is small, but it clearly gave a benefit to the test results.

However, compared with the overlap of the training sets provided with the benchmarks, the overlap was not noticeably larger.

> Understanding and quantifying how highly similar text impacts performance is an important research question. Better de-duplication techniques such as scalable fuzzy matching could also help better answer these questions. For now, we recommend the use of n-gram overlap based de-duplication as an important verification step and sanity check during the creation of training and test splits for new NLP datasets.

Understanding and quantifying how much similar text affects performance is an important research question, and better de-duplication techniques will give better answers. The authors recommend using n-gram-overlap-based de-duplication when splitting new NLP datasets into training and test sets.

## 7. Discussion

This paper has an unusual order: Related Work, which normally comes right after the Introduction, comes just before the Discussion. Related Work lists other datasets and earlier pre-trained models that worked well with fine-tuning, so I'll skip it.

The GPT-2 paper devotes a considerable amount of space to the Discussion.

That's because, as the experimental results revealed, the limits of GPT-2's zero-shot performance are so obvious that it can't be used in practice (this is also the decisive reason GPT-2 never took off compared with BERT and its derivatives).

So they address whether, since zero-shot learning alone falls short, they will turn toward fine-tuning, or whether they see room for improvement.

> Much research has been dedicated to learning (Hill et al., 2016), understanding (Levy & Goldberg, 2014), and critically evaluating (Wieting & Kiela, 2019) the representations of both supervised and unsupervised pre-training methods. Our results suggest that unsupervised task learning is an additional promising area of research to explore. These findings potentially help explain the widespread success of pre-training techniques for down-stream NLP tasks as we show that, in the limit, one of these pre-training techniques begins to learn to perform tasks directly without the need for supervised adaption or modification.

They say their results present unsupervised task learning as a promising new area of research. Their findings help explain why pre-training techniques succeed on downstream tasks, and they showed that, although with limits, a pre-training technique takes the first step toward learning to perform tasks directly without supervised fine-tuning or model modification.

> On reading comprehension the performance of GPT-2 is competitive with supervised baselines in a zero-shot setting. However, on other tasks such as summarization, while it is qualitatively performing the task, its performance is still only rudimentary according to quantitative metrics. While suggestive as a research result, in terms of practical applications, the zero-shot performance of GPT-2 is still far from use-able.

In reading comprehension, zero-shot GPT-2 was competitive with fine-tuned baselines.

But on other tasks such as summarization, zero-shot may have performed the task qualitatively, yet by quantitative metrics it was still at the baby-steps stage. Research results aside, GPT-2's zero-shot performance still has a long way to go for practical applications.

> We have studied the zero-shot performance of WebText LMs on many canonical NLP tasks, but there are many additional tasks that could be evaluated. There are undoubtedly many practical tasks where the performance of GPT-2 is still no better than random. Even on common tasks that we evaluated on, such as question answering and translation, language models only begin to outperform trivial baselines when they have sufficient capacity.

They have checked the zero-shot performance of WebText language models on many NLP tasks, but many more tasks remain to be evaluated. There are also many tasks where GPT-2 does no better than random.

Even on common tasks such as question answering and translation, the models only barely beat trivial baselines once they had sufficient capacity (a huge size).

> While zero-shot performance establishes a baseline of the potential performance of GPT-2 on many tasks, it is not clear where the ceiling is with finetuning. On some tasks, GPT-2’s fully abstractive output is a significant departure from the extractive pointer network (Vinyals et al., 2015) based outputs which are currently state of the art on many question answering and reading comprehension datasets.

Although zero-shot performance establishes a baseline for what GPT-2 can do on many tasks, it is unclear how high GPT-2's ceiling is when fine-tuning is added.

On some tasks, GPT-2's fully abstractive output differs significantly from the outputs of extractive pointer networks, which are currently SOTA on QA and RC.

> Given the prior success of fine-tuning GPT, we plan to investigate fine-tuning on benchmarks such as decaNLP and GLUE, especially since it is unclear whether the additional training data and capacity of GPT-2 is sufficient to overcome the inefficiencies of uni-directional representations demonstrated by BERT (Devlin et al., 2018).

Since fine-tuning GPT succeeded before, they plan to try fine-tuning on benchmarks like decaNLP and GLUE. This is because it is still unknown whether GPT-2, trained with more data and at a larger size, can overcome the inefficiency of uni-directional representations demonstrated in the 2018 BERT paper.

This is an interesting paragraph. For one thing, it gives the impression that the GPT-2 authors may stop insisting on zero-shot and compromise with fine-tuning. For another, it is a rebuttal to BERT's criticism of GPT's uni-directionality.

For reference, the Introduction of the BERT paper points out the limitation of GPT's uni-directionality as follows.

> The major limitation is that standard language models are uni-directional, and this limits the choice of architectures that can be used during pre-training. For example, in OpenAI GPT, the authors use a left-to-right architecture, where every token can only attend to previous tokens in the self-attention layers of the Transformer (Vaswani et al., 2017). Such restrictions are sub-optimal for sentence-level tasks, and could be very harmful when applying fine-tuning based approaches to token-level tasks such as question answering, where it is crucial to incorporate context from both directions.

It says that a model with a uni-directional constraint like OpenAI GPT is fatal when fine-tuned on token-level tasks such as question answering, which need context from both directions.

The GPT-2 authors hit back that the problem BERT pointed out was with GPT, and that a fine-tuned GPT-2 might be a different story.

If GPT raised expectations for zero-shot learning, actually doing multitasking with GPT-2 was disappointing. Was OpenAI able to restore its honor? Let's find out together next time. Coming soon.

## 8. Conclusion

> When a large language model is trained on a sufficiently large and diverse dataset it is able to perform well across many domains and datasets. GPT-2 zero-shots to state of the art performance on 7 out of 8 tested language modeling datasets. The diversity of tasks the model is able to perform in a zero-shot setting suggests that high-capacity models trained to maximize the likelihood of a sufficiently varied text corpus begin to learn how to perform a surprising amount of tasks without the need for explicit supervision.

If you train a large language model on a sufficiently large and diverse dataset, it can work well on downstream tasks regardless of domain or benchmark dataset.

GPT-2's zero-shot performance hit SOTA on 7 of the 8 benchmarks tested.

The fact that the model can do a variety of tasks zero-shot

shows that high-capacity language models trained on diverse corpora with a maximum-likelihood objective learn how to do tasks (while doing inference) without supervision.
